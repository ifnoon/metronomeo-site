How to practice 3:2 and 4:3 polyrhythms

By Metronomeo · 17 November 2026 · 5 min read

A polyrhythm places different, evenly spaced rhythms over the same span of time. In a 3:2 exercise, one part plays three pulses while another plays two. They begin together, separate, and meet again at the next cycle. The useful first task is hearing both parts clearly without turning either one into an uneven approximation of the other.

You can practice this with two hands, a hand and a foot, or your voice against a repeated instrumental pattern. Start quietly and slowly. A familiar tapping movement makes it easier to concentrate on the relationship rather than technique.

Understand what the ratio means

For this guide, the first number describes the rhythm you are adding and the second describes your reference beats. Three against two means three equal pulses across two reference beats. Four against three means four equal pulses across three reference beats. Both parts cover the same complete cycle.

This is different from playing a triplet inside every beat. If you play three notes inside each of two beats, you get six notes over the cycle. Three against two has only three pulses over those same two beats. Establish which span you are dividing before counting faster notes.

Put 3:2 on a shared grid

Divide one complete cycle into six equal spaces. The two pulse part lands at positions one and four. The three pulse part lands at positions one, three and five. Both start together at position one; the next shared attack is the beginning of the following cycle.

The combined pattern is not a stream of equally spaced attacks. There are two small spaces from the shared start to the next attack, then one, then one, then two to the next shared start. Keep the individual parts even instead of flattening that combined pattern into four equal taps.

  1. Count six evenly spaced positions slowly. Tap your left hand on one and four.
  2. Keep counting and tap your right hand on one, three and five.
  3. Alternate listening to the left part and the right part. Neither should change when your attention moves.
  4. Stop counting the small grid and hear two slow pulses beneath three flowing pulses.
  5. Switch hands. A pattern that works only with your stronger hand may need another slow repetition.

The diagram and audio in this guide use a lower sound for the two reference beats and a higher sound for the three pulses. Listen once before tapping. The sounds are a timing illustration, not a recording of the app or a test of your playing.

Set up 3:2 in Metronomeo

Long press the upper row and set two beats, then long press the lower row and set three pulses. Confirm each count with the checkmark in the dial. Tap the polyrhythm icon immediately left of Help so its circle is filled.

With polyrhythm enabled, the three lower pulses span the whole two beat measure. With it disabled, the lower row repeats three divisions inside each upper beat. The counts stay the same when you toggle; their relationship changes. Listen for that difference before beginning an exercise.

Start around 60 BPM if that feels comfortable. The upper beats are your tempo reference. Use a short tap to mute a pulse when you want to hear the other row alone, then restore it. You do not need to change the count or tempo to isolate a part.

Move from 3:2 to 4:3

Four against three uses a twelve space grid. The three reference beats land at positions one, five and nine. The four pulses land at one, four, seven and ten. Keep the reference beats evenly spaced even though the other part repeatedly lands between them.

In Metronomeo, set three upper beats and four lower pulses, with polyrhythm enabled. First listen to the three beat part alone. Restore the other row and tap the four pulse part with one hand while keeping three with your foot. Slow down if either part becomes a series of guesses.

Do not assume that mastering 3:2 automatically makes 4:3 comfortable. The larger grid gives you a different sequence of distances between attacks. Work with a complete cycle, not just its first few notes.

Bring the pattern to your instrument

For drums, keep the reference with a foot and play the other rhythm on one surface. Add orchestration only when the spacing is reliable. For piano, try one repeated note in each hand before using chords. Guitarists and bassists can sing one part while playing the other, or leave the reference to the metronome.

Play several cycles without a fill, then add one small variation. Listen to the next shared beginning. If the variation makes the two parts meet at the wrong time, simplify it rather than forcing the final attack into place.

Check the right thing

Each part should sound even when heard alone. Both should cover the same cycle. Their shared beginning should return consistently. Those three checks are more useful than whether the combined pattern sounds impressive at a high tempo.

Once the relationship feels natural, apply it to a short musical phrase. Keep a clear reference and leave space to hear what the other rhythm is doing. More notes are not necessary to make the exercise useful.
